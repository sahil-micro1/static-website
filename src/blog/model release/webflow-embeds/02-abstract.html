Abstract

As reasoning models become more capable, a central challenge in reinforcement-learning post-training is designing tasks that remain challenging while providing reliable feedback under finite sampling compute budgets. We investigate this data-efficiency challenge using 1,600 distinct expert-written STEM problems to post-train DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Our recipe combines correctness-based feedback, reward shaping, and an adaptive curriculum that allocates sampling tokens according to observed success and response length. After 25 RL training steps, our model’s correctness increases from 36.40% to 39.84% on Humanity’s Last Exam, from 75.40% to 77.35% on FrontierScience Olympiad, and from 16.83% to 19.11% on FrontierScience Research. Improvements also extend to long-document reasoning in business and government domains: AA-LCR v1.1 accuracy rises from 80.00% to 85.00% following post-training on the expert STEM dataset. Among 44 held-out STEM questions with no accepted answer in eight baseline attempts, 11 received at least one accepted answer in eight post-training attempts. To examine what makes these problems useful for training, we define learnability: a task’s potential to support improvement for a particular model at a given training stage. We distinguish this potential from observed correctness and within-group reward contrast. The results suggest that expert-data training utility depends on task design, grading consistency, and matching tasks to the model’s current learning needs. Together, these components support more reliable performance while preserving opportunities for progress on harder problems.

1 Introduction

Reasoning training is a promising foundation for broader agentic post-training [1, 2], but continued progress requires challenging tasks with useful feedback despite costly trajectories and limited outcome signals when attempts consistently succeed or fail [6]. We test whether a small expert dataset can improve an already strong model when task design and curriculum match its current learning needs, post-training the 748B-parameter DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on 1,600 distinct STEM tasks written and reviewed by PhD experts. We define learnability as a task’s potential to improve a particular model at a given training stage; clear requirements, verified references, and reliable grading support this potential, while difficulty and observed success alone do not establish learning value.

Our learnability-adaptive curriculum uses success and response length to allocate generation tokens across mastery, frontier practice, and exploration within expert data organized by domain and difficulty. It prioritizes mixed-success tasks while consolidating emerging skills and revisiting harder problems as learning needs change. Over the first 25 steps, groups with zero relative advantage after reward shaping account for 10.69% under the curriculum recipe versus 45.56% under the uniform recipe; these recipes differ in settings and generation budgets, so the comparison does not isolate the curriculum’s effect.

After 25 steps of rank-32 LoRA RL, Humanity’s Last Exam accuracy rises from 36.40% to 39.84%: 86 net additional correct answers across 2,500 questions, with one generated response per question. Gains extend to held-out STEM questions, both FrontierScience splits, and AA-LCR v1.1, which includes business and government reports [7] and improves from 80.00% to 85.00%. Among 44 held-out STEM questions with no accepted answer in eight baseline attempts, 11—one quarter—receive at least one accepted answer in eight post-training attempts. These findings support the combined recipe, designed to make occasional success more reliable while preserving opportunities on harder problems, with learnability guiding expert task design, grading, and adaptive practice.

2 Background

Prior work motivates reasoning as a foundation for broader post-training: outcome-based RL in DeepSeek-R1 [1], reasoning with interaction in ReAct [9], and mixed STEM and agentic training in MAI-Thinking-1 [2] make the training more stable. Expert benchmarks highlight frontier-level challenge [5], while long reasoning trajectories make informative feedback costly to obtain; process supervision studies step-level feedback [10], and DAPO addresses groups without reward contrast [6]. Evidence of distribution sharpening [11, 12] sits alongside broader observed coverage after prolonged RL [13] and reduced sharpening from rewarding unlikely successes in theorem proving [14]. Curriculum learning studies example selection and ordering [15], while automated curricula adapt practice to learning progress and cost [16]. These themes motivate our focus on learnability, a task’s potential to improve a particular model at a given stage, and on expert STEM data with aligned references and grading. Our adaptive curriculum uses observed success and response length to allocate generation tokens across mastery, frontier practice, and exploration.

3 Methods

3.1 Training configuration and data

We post-train DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with rank-32 LoRA for 25 steps, using 64 question groups per update and a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁵. A question group contains multiple responses to the same problem. Training uses routing replay and asynchronous updates with at most one update of policy lag. The run uses 1,600 distinct expert-written training problems. The problem pool remains fixed during the run while the allocation of generation changes.

3.2 Expert tasks and reference grading

The question, expert reference solution, and grading criteria specify a common target. The question defines the task and its constraints, the worked solution explains a valid way to solve it, and the grader checks whether the model’s answer meets those requirements. These tasks target accurate problem interpretation, application of constraints, and complete answers.

Our companion study, STEM QA Frontier, documents how experts develop and review tasks [3]. Doctorate-holding domain experts write each question, its reference answer, and a worked solution. They label the subject area, manually grade responses from frontier models, and document identified failures. A second expert checks the solution, assumptions, required precision, and answer requirements. Questions with unclear requirements or competing valid answers are revised or removed. An LLM-based grader then compares the model’s final answer with the reference, accepting different forms of the same answer while checking the required precision. Together, these steps help make each task a dependable source of training feedback, grounded in a clear, expert-reviewed standard of correctness.

3.3 Correctness feedback and group-relative updates

The model generates its own responses, which may include exact values, mathematical expressions, scientific structures, or lists of numerical values. Each response receives a binary correctness grade from the reference-based grader. Correctness therefore depends on satisfying the task’s answer requirements, including equivalence and numerical precision where applicable.

Training uses a group-relative approach: differences among response rewards within a question group provide contrast for the update [6]. We distinguish binary correctness grades from the rewards after shaping. In mastery practice, the reward also favors shorter responses when every answer in the group is correct. Identical correctness grades provide no correctness contrast, although this brevity preference can distinguish rewards within a fully correct mastery group. The zero-advantage diagnostic below is measured after reward shaping.

3.4 Adaptive allocation of rollout budget

The curriculum updates success and response-length estimates from earlier outcomes and uses them to allocate generation across three practice roles. Frontier practice concentrates on problems with mixed observed success; exploration retains attempts on harder problems; mastery revisits reliably solved problems. Response-length estimates account for unequal generation costs across problems and group sizes.

The target allocation is 70% of generated tokens to frontier practice, 15% to exploration, and 15% to mastery, using 16, 32, and 8 responses per group, respectively. These are token-allocation targets, not fractions of questions or measurements of realized allocation. New outcomes inform subsequent sampling from the fixed problem pool.

4 Experimental setup

We compare the DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash base model with the checkpoint after 25 post-training steps. Scores average correctness across individual answers. Repeated answers estimate single-attempt performance; no voting or model-side tools are used.

The STEMQA evaluation uses a 188-question same-source holdout that shares no question identifiers with the 1,600 training problems. It was stratified by domain and historical success rate within an earlier source cohort, before the training pool was expanded. Table 1 distinguishes unique questions from sampled answers.

STEMQA shares the effort-100 instruction, nominal sampling settings, and grader across models; the base uses chat prompts and checkpoints use pretokenized continuation prompts. Its before/after comparison is descriptive across those execution paths. HLE and AA-LCR use matched recorded generation settings. Graders are GLM-5.3-Flash for STEMQA, o3-mini for HLE, GPT-5 for FrontierScience, and GPT-5.6-Luna for AA-LCR. FrontierScience Research accepts scores of at least 7/10.

5 Results

The update-25 checkpoint scores higher on HLE, both FrontierScience splits, and AA-LCR. Figure 1 summarizes benchmark results. On the same-source STEMQA holdout, observed correctness increases from 51.26% to 64.69%; this comparison includes the execution-path difference specified above.

5.1 Humanity’s Last Exam

HLE tests technical knowledge and reasoning across more than 100 subjects, using challenging questions written by experts [5].

HLE correctness rises from 36.40% to 39.84%: 86 net additional correct answers across 2,500 questions. The gains appear in seven of eight benchmark categories, with mathematics contributing the largest number of additional correct answers.

5.2 FrontierScience

Olympiad improves from 75.40% to 77.35%; Research improves from 16.83% to 19.11%. These evaluations extend the observed gains beyond the same-source STEMQA holdout to independent scientific-reasoning benchmarks.

5.3 Long-document reasoning on AA-LCR

AA-LCR v1.1 accuracy increases from 80.00% to 85.00%, a gain of 5.00 percentage points. The benchmark tests reasoning over long documents [7]. In this evaluation, correctness increases in company reports, government consultations, academic papers, industry reports, and marketing materials; legal and survey questions have unchanged aggregate scores. These descriptive category results extend the observed gains beyond the format of the STEMQA training tasks.

5.4 Training dynamics for the complete recipes

Across the first 25 steps, zero-advantage groups after reward shaping account for 45.56% under the uniform recipe (729 of 1,600 groups) and 10.69% under the curriculum recipe (171 of 1,600 groups). Generated training tokens total 673.5 million and 1.27 billion, respectively, a 1.89-fold difference.

The recipes also differ in response-group size, learning rate, replay, update scheduling, reward shaping, and sampled question mix. The uniform run uses eight responses per group, a 10⁻⁵ learning rate, synchronous updates, and no routing replay. The curriculum recipe uses 16/32/8 responses for frontier/exploration/mastery, a 3 × 10⁻⁵ learning rate, routing replay, asynchronous updates with at most one update of lag, and mastery-group brevity shaping. The diagnostics show that the complete curriculum recipe produces fewer zero-advantage groups under its sampling distribution. This unequal-budget comparison does not isolate the curriculum’s contribution or estimate improvement per token.

6 Selected data case studies

To examine changes in individual answers, we compare eight attempts per STEMQA question before and after training. Among 44 held-out questions with no accepted base answer, 11 receive at least one accepted answer after training, accounting for 31 additional accepted answers. We reviewed all 176 responses from the two models on these 11 questions. This count uses the original grader acceptances, including some items later flagged for reference or explanation issues. The two independently verified STEMQA examples below illustrate requirements for reference solutions and grading; they are selected supporting analyses.

A 0/8 result means that no accepted answer was observed in eight attempts, not that the model is unable to solve the problem. The cases describe the recorded answers and their requirements, rather than demonstrating a general change in reasoning strategy.

6.2 Physics: Completing a multi-step numerical calculation

A three-flavor neutrino-oscillation problem tests whether a model can carry a multi-step calculation through to a complete numerical answer. It combines propagation through four specified layers, two conditional layer choices, dephasing, and averaging across three energy bins. The required output contains ten values: six neutrino and antineutrino flavor probabilities, their three differences, and the norm of those differences. Every entry must independently fall within 1% of its reference value.

The selected base response satisfies 8 of the 10 checks, but misses the electron-antineutrino probability and its corresponding neutrino–antineutrino difference. The selected trained response satisfies all 10. Both give the same acceptable norm, 0.473: checking that summary alone would conceal the base response’s two errors. Across eight independent attempts per model, none of the base answers but one trained answer satisfy the complete numerical target.

Independent high-precision matrix calculations reproduce the reference values and confirm that the selected response after training meets every numerical requirement. The successful saved response contains only the final tuple, so it establishes a correct numerical outcome without showing which intermediate step changed. This illustrates why the grader must check each requested value: an answer can be mostly accurate and still fail to satisfy the complete task.

6.3 An HLE example: track the requested object

A quantum-information question asks for the maximum Kraus rank of the complementary channel of a Pauli channel on a d-dimensional system. The base answers d²; the trained model answers d. The base counts the original channel’s possible Pauli operators. The trained answer instead traces out the d-dimensional output, giving at most d Kraus operators for the complement. An independent algebraic check confirms this bound is attainable. The difference is applying the constraint to the object the question actually asks about.
