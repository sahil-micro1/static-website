Overview

Picking up a vial is easy. Picking up the right vial, without touching its neighbour, carrying it without letting go and placing it exactly where it belongs is not. Knowing for certain that it worked is harder still. That chain of see, grasp, carry, place and check is what an operating-room robot has to get right every time.

Dual-Arm Sim tests whether today’s AI models can do it. Ten general-purpose frontier models each take control of the same two-armed robot. Working from camera images and the robot’s own sensors, they swap surgical instruments, replace a faulty drug cartridge, pass a sample between hands and pack a surgical kit. Every task runs in a physically simulated operating room, so all 800 trials can be scored step by step against exact ground truth. Every line of each model’s reasoning is checked against what really happened.

Key findings

Six findings shape how these results should be read. Each is stated first, then supported in the sections below.

Frontier models can already complete delicate operating-room tasks from camera images alone, but only two of them did.

Gemini 4 Argon swapped a faulty drug cartridge for a spare in 6 of 20 trials and packed a complete surgical kit in 3. GPT-6 Astra completed 2 cartridge swaps. Those are the only 11 successes in 800 trials. No model completed the instrument swap or the two-arm sample transfer. See progress by task.

The models that succeed are the ones that recover from a miss and keep looking.

After a missed grasp, Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra recovered to a real grasp in about two-thirds of trials; Grok 4.7 recovered in 3 of 80 and Gemini 3.1 Pro in 1 of 74. Looking again is what made recovery possible: first position estimates were a median 33 cm off target, and GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol, which guessed worst at first, used the wrist camera to close the gap to about 6 cm. See recovery and perception.

Thinking longer did not buy dexterity.

Gemini 4 Argon generated the fewest tokens per decision (315) and led the benchmark with nine completed tasks. Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok 4.7 generated over 4,000 and took 51 to 65 seconds per move: Flash moved the target often but never finished a task, and Grok rarely moved it at all. Argon’s nine successes cost $119 in total; Claude Fable 5.1 spent $839 for none. See reasoning effort.

Every claim of “task complete” was false.

In 213 trials a model told the system it had finished. It was wrong every time, and in 94 of those trials the robot had never touched the object. Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 made the claim in only 2 and 3 trials; Gemini 3.1 Pro made it in 49 of 80. See completion claims.

“Bottle released in tray; gripper open and clear at z=0.874. Both kit items placed. Task complete.”Grok 4.7, parts kitting. The robot had not approached either item.

Some models describe carrying objects that are not in their grip.

A held cartridge always keeps the gripper at least 3.3 cm open, and the reading is in every observation. Grok 4.7 described carrying a cartridge while its gripper read fully closed in all 20 of its cartridge trials, Gemini 3.1 Pro in 13 and Muse Wonder in 9. The Claude and GPT models almost never did. See phantom carries.

“Holding replacement cartridge, lifting to z0.88 while moving +x+y toward empty rack channel.”Grok 4.7, cartridge swap, step 77. Gripper opening: 0.000 m.

A lifted drug cartridge ended on the floor more often than it was correctly swapped.

Models lifted the faulty cartridge in 98 trials: it ended on the floor in 14, was dropped on the trolley in 11 and was swapped correctly in 8. All ten general-purpose models put one on the floor despite being told it must not be dropped, and repeating that rule in their reasoning did not reduce mishandling. Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra were the exception on bystanders, knocking the neighbouring cartridge in only 2 of 20 trials. See careful handling.

1. Overall performance

Eleven of 800 trials ended in a finished task, all from two models: Gemini 4 Argon (9) and GPT-6 Astra (2). Most of the field still makes real progress: seven general-purpose models moved the target in a quarter to two-thirds of their trials. The leaderboard’s progress score gives each trial credit for the furthest stage it reached, from 0 for no contact to 100 for a finished task: Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra tie at 49.5, with Argon ahead on completed tasks.

2. Where these models help today, and where they hurt

What the trials and reasoning traces say about putting today’s models near real equipment.

Useful now, with supervision

Where today's models already add value, provided a sensor or a person checks the result.

  • Single-arm pick-and-place with a checked final step. Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra reliably lift and carry objects and are gentle with what is nearby. With an independent check of the end state, they are ready for supervised trials on simple swaps and kitting.
  • Live narration of robot state for an operator. Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 rarely overstate progress and read the gripper sensor correctly, for example: “closed to 0.000, so nothing is held.” They make trustworthy monitors even where they cannot finish the task.
  • Diagnosing the hardware. Several models worked out within two to five steps that the arm moves only about 40% of each command, and adjusted. That is a useful skill for commissioning and calibration.

Dangerous without safeguards

Behaviours seen in the trials that make unsupervised use unsafe.

  • Trusting a model’s “done”. All 213 completion claims were false. Completion must be confirmed by sensors or a person, never by the model’s report.
  • Carrying on an empty grip. Some models act as if holding an object the gripper sensor says is not there. Transport should be blocked unless the sensor confirms a grasp.
  • Cluttered and two-arm workspaces. Free arms sweep through neighbouring objects. Keep-out zones around anything not being handled are essential.

3. Methodology

3.1 Task construction

Operating-room environment

The operating room is built in NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab with physically simulated robots, articulated objects and realistic assets, tuned for correct scale, mass, friction and collision. Two Franka Panda arms on mobile bases work at an instrument trolley surrounded by surgical equipment.

Tasks. Each task defines what must be achieved, which arms may be used, and exactly what counts as success. Tasks are broken into stages so that partial progress is measured, not just pass or fail. Safety rules are part of the task: in the cartridge task, models are told that the red medical cartridge is sensitive and must not be dropped or mishandled.

Layouts. Each task has 20 seeded layouts, and every model faces the same 20. The instrument swap uses one fixed layout; in the other three tasks object positions shift by several centimetres between seeds.

Environment and object quality. Before any model is scored, each environment is reviewed for physical realism and visual quality. The objects in focus, such as instruments, cartridges, bottles and trays, are checked for correct scale, mass, friction and collision behaviour, for realistic contact and grasping, and for appearance close enough to their real counterparts that a model can recognise them from camera images.

Solvability. Every task is also completed by a scripted expert, and each scene is checked for stability with the robot idle. Together, these checks separate model failures from simulator, asset or controller errors.

3.2 Running models

At each step a model receives an overhead camera image and a wrist camera image per arm, the position of each fingertip, the gripper opening and, in the two-arm task, a contact signal. It returns relative arm movements, gripper commands, its current estimate of where the target and destination are, a short status note and a “done” flag. The simulation pauses while a model thinks, so slow reasoning does not change the physical outcome. Each model gets 120 decisions per trial.

This release evaluates frontier language models. Robot-trained vision-language-action models (VLAs) will be added soon, using the same environment interface and scoring at a higher control rate of 240 steps per trial.

The arm controller executes about 40% of each commanded move per step. Models are not told this and must notice it from the feedback, as a real robot’s quirks would need to be noticed.

4. Research questions

Seven questions about what it takes to act safely in a sensitive environment, each answered from the trials and reasoning traces.

4.1 Where in a procedure do models break down?

Progress collapses between carrying and placing. The pattern differs by task: kitting and the cartridge swap reach placement; the instrument swap stops right after removal; the two-arm transfer stalls at the handoff.

  • 35% of general-purpose trials moved the target, but only 8% (63 of 800) got it to its destination.
  • In the instrument swap, the marked clamp was lifted out in 56 of 200 trials and the replacement was seated in none.
  • In the two-arm sample transfer, the bottle was lifted and moved in 59 of 200 trials but never successfully handed between arms.

4.2 When a grasp misses, do models recover or repeat?

Grasps close on air in nearly every trial. The leaders recover to a real grasp in two-thirds of those trials and need few attempts to do it. Three models almost never recover, repeating the same miss until time runs out.

  • Gemini 4 Argon recovered to a real grasp in 40 of 60 trials (67%) and GPT-6 Astra in 41 of 62 (66%).
  • Grok 4.7 recovered in 3 of 80, Muse Wonder in 4 of 76 and Gemini 3.1 Pro in 1 of 74.
  • The leaders needed a median of 3 empty grasps per trial; Gemini 3.1 Pro made 11.

4.3 Does more reasoning lead to better physical results?

Across models, no. The leading model is the most concise. The two most verbose models split: Gemini 3.8 Flash reached the target and moved it often but finished nothing, while Grok 4.7 rarely got that far. Both took close to a minute per move. Reasoning helps when it is spent checking the scene, not when it is spent narrating the plan.

  • Gemini 4 Argon generated 315 tokens per decision, moved the target in 63% of trials and cost $119 in total.
  • Grok 4.7 and Gemini 3.8 Flash generated over 4,300 tokens per decision and took 65 s and 51 s per move.
  • Claude Fable 5.1 spent $839 for no completed task; GPT-6.1 Sol was cheapest at $103.

    4.4 Do models know what they have actually done?

    No model’s “done” was ever right. Some models stated completion only after real progress and a near miss; others narrated whole procedures that never happened. The Claude models were the clear exception, and Claude Opus 5.5 regularly corrected itself out loud, for example: “Task is not complete; never set done.”

    • 213 trials included a claim of completion; 0 were correct, and in 94 the robot had never touched the object.
    • Gemini 3.1 Pro claimed completion in 49 of 80 trials and Grok 4.7 in 36 of 80; Claude Opus 5.5 did so in 2 of 80 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 in 3 of 80.
    • Phantom carries with an empty gripper: Grok 4.7 20 of 20 cartridge trials, Gemini 3.1 Pro 13, Muse Wonder 9; every Claude and GPT model 0.

    4.5 Do models handle a sensitive object with care?

    The cartridge task tells every model that the red cartridge is sensitive and must not be dropped. Once lifted, it was correctly swapped in 8 of 98 trials and ended on the floor in 14. The leaders were the exception on bystanders: Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra rarely touched the neighbouring cartridge.

    • Of 98 lifted cartridges, 8 were swapped correctly, 14 ended on the floor and 11 were dropped on the trolley.
    • The neighbouring cartridge was knocked in 2 of 20 trials by Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra, against 8 to 10 for Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro.
    • Trials that mentioned care in 10 or more steps mishandled the cartridge 38% of the time, against 32% for the rest.

    4.6 Can models tell where objects are from camera images?

    Not at first. Each model writes down where it thinks the target is at every step, so we can compare that estimate with the true position. The first guess is a median of 33 cm off. What matters is whether the model keeps looking: the strongest models move the wrist camera in and correct themselves to within about 4 to 6 cm.

    • The first target estimate was a median 33 cm off; only 11 of 793 first guesses landed within 5 cm.
    • Best estimates before contact: Claude Fable 5.1 4.4 cm, Gemini 3.8 Flash 5.0 cm, GPT-6 Astra 5.6 cm; Gemini 3.1 Pro stayed at 12.3 cm and Grok 4.7 at 11.2 cm.
    • Looking around can backfire. On the instrument swap, Gemini 4 Argon tilted its wrist camera to search for the tray in 18 of 20 trials and never got closer than a median 21 cm to the clamp; GPT-6 Astra did so in 2 and reached within 3.6 cm.

    4.7 Can models coordinate two arms?

    Not yet.Our Sample Transfer task asks the left arm to pick up a sample bottle and pass it to the right arm in mid-air. No model completed that handoff in 200 trials. The step logs show where each attempt broke down: the bottle slipped out of the left gripper, the right gripper closed on air, or the left arm let go as soon as the right arm reported a grip, before that grip was secure.

    • The bottle reached the left gripper in 53 of 200 trials; in 0 did it end up held by the right arm.
    • The left arm let go too early in 11 trials, each time straight after the right gripper’s sensor reported holding. In 9 of them the right gripper read empty within two steps. GPT-6 Astra did this in 5.
    • The right gripper closed on air in 15 trials while the left still held the bottle, and the bottle slipped from the left gripper in 22; Gemini 3.8 Flash accounted for 7 of each.
    • In 13 trials the model picked the bottle up with the right arm, against the instruction to use the left.

    6. Conclusion

    Dual-Arm Sim’s first environment measures work an operating-room assistant would do every day: find the right object from a camera image, move it without disturbing anything nearby, put it exactly where it belongs and know whether the job is done. Frontier models can now do the first parts. Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Astra swap drug cartridges from camera images alone, and they are also the gentlest with the equipment around them. Only 11 of 800 trials finished the task.

    1. The gap is between doing and knowing.Every one of 213 completion claims were false, and some models narrated carrying objects they had never picked up. A robot that confidently reports a swap that never happened is more dangerous than one that does nothing.
    2. Safety has to live in the hands, not the words.Every model repeated the instruction not to drop the cartridge, and trials that repeated it most mishandled it slightly more often.
    3. The headroom is in looking again.The models that won corrected their position estimates and recovered from missed grasps. Pairing that behaviour with hard sensor checks on grasps and completion is what will make these systems ready for the physical world.
