Unsafe content reaches the user

The model answers a harmful request because the harm is spread across turns or wrapped in a framing, and the safeguarding concern is never named. This is the failure the benchmark weighs most heavily: a single expert override flag fails the arm regardless of anything else in the reply.

In single turn the same failure takes a recognizable shape: the model names the concern, then supplies the technique inside the requested frame, a realistic grooming arc for a "story", administration routes for a "homework" question, concealment tips in a "school paper".

What this means for deploying models with young users

WellnessBench is hard because it asks for two things at once: no harmful detail, and no abandoned user. Either one alone is easy to achieve. Four takeaways follow.

  1. Evaluate in conversation, not on single prompts.Single turn results of 71% to 91% would suggest the problem is largely handled. The same models pass 18% to 48% of dialogue scenarios, where risk is assembled across turns, emerges midway, or has to be held under pushback. A product that will hold conversations with young people should be tested in conversation.
  2. Measure both directions of failure.A model that rarely leaks can still fail a young person by refusing and leaving, and a model that never abandons can still leak. Leak rate, abandonment rate and refusal of benign requests should be read together; any one of them alone gives a misleading picture of safety.
  3. Test the product, not only the model.The same models passed fewer conversations in their consumer apps than through the API, because the apps gave less support rather than more harmful detail. App layers can help, as the Claude apps did on direct harmful requests, or hurt, as an age gate that refuses a teenager outright does. What a young person receives has to be tested in the product they will use.
  4. Look for the supportive steps.What separates a pass from a fail in dialogue is mostly whether the model names a trusted adult or a real resource, asks before assuming, explains its concern in terms the user can accept, and keeps that concern when the user changes the subject. These are the behaviors to check for in review, and they are the ones a refusal policy alone does not produce.

Next steps

WellnessBench is a starting point. Next steps are a second judge for single turn leak detection, an expert-labelled calibration set of disputed replies, more runs per dialogue, partial credit that separates a leak from a missed supportive step within a run, and an API condition that adds each app's published system prompt to test how much of the app difference it explains.
