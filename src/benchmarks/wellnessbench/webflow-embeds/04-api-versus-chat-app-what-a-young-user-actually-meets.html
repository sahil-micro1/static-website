API versus chat app: what a young user actually meets

The scoreboard and the analysis that follows measure the models through their APIs, with no system prompt. A young person meets these models differently: through a consumer chat app, which adds its own system prompt, safety layer and settings. To see whether that changes the result, we relayed a set of WellnessBench scenarios by hand through six chat apps: the Claude app for Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Sonnet 5.5, the Gemini app for Gemini 3.8 Flash, ChatGPT for GPT-6 Sol and the Grok app for Grok 4.7. As with the API runs, the simulated user was partly scripted and intent-guided, with each turn adapted by Gemini 3.8 Flash. The response window gate and the judge were the same on both sides, and each app run was compared task by task with the API runs on the same scenario version.

Automated browsing of the apps was ruled out because their terms forbid it, so every conversation was typed in and copied back by a person. GPT-5.6 Sol was not run in the apps.

The difference is clear, and the direction matters. Through the apps, models passed fewer primary conversations than through the API, 28% against 39% (p = 0.008). The apps did not give out more harmful details. They gave less support: replies more often left out the guidance and reassurance the clinicians require, and some apps refused outright and left the user with nothing. Both are safety failures, and WellnessBench scores both as fails.

Every app is weaker in conversation

The gap sits in dialogue, and it runs the same way for all six models. It is largest for Claude Opus 5.5, which passes 11% of dialogue primaries in the Claude app against 44% through the API, a real gap on its own, and smallest for Grok 4.7 at 19% against 21%. On single turn scenarios the pooled result evens out but splits by vendor: the Claude apps declined direct harmful requests more often than the API, while the Grok app did worse than its API.

Less harm, less support

Splitting the failures shows what changes in the app. Harmful detail is about as common in the apps as through the API, 15% of primaries against 18%. What rises is the failure to support the user: 58% of app primaries miss at least one supportive step the clinicians require, such as encouraging a trusted adult, offering a real support option or reassuring the user, against 40% through the API. Replies that refuse and drop the user rise from 2% to 6%.

  2. Length does not explain it.The Claude apps and Gemini wrote shorter replies than their APIs, but ChatGPT wrote about three times as much as GPT-6 Sol through the API and Grok about 1.7 times as much, and both still missed more supportive steps.
  3. Harmful detail moves in different directions by model provider.It falls in the app for Claude Fable 5.1, from 33% to 6%, a real gap, and rises for GPT-6 Sol and Grok 4.7, though neither rise is a real gap on its own. Pooled, the movements cancel out.
  4. Dropping the user mostly comes from canned refusals.Most app primaries judged as abandoning the user are a one line refusal: the Gemini app's "I'm having a hard time fulfilling your request" or the Grok app's "I can't discuss that as you're under 18". A teenager who discloses a problem and receives one of these is left with nothing, so they are graded as failures, exactly as the same reply would be through the API.

Audit of the relayed conversations

Relaying by hand introduces its own errors, so every conversation was checked twice. Scripted checks looked for a control continued in the primary's chat, details from the primary appearing in the control, duplicated replies, replies matching another scenario better than their own, and reply timing and operator notes. An AI reviewer then read every app run blind, mixed with API runs as decoys; it flagged none of the decoys. Eight app runs were blocked by the reviewer's own content filter, and their control conversations were read by hand. Every flag was confirmed against the transcript.

How to read this check

The pooled result is the finding; the per model figures are directional and should not be ranked. Each model rests on one app run per scenario, so only large gaps can register on their own. Where a model is singled out, it is for a pattern with a clear mechanism: the Claude apps declining direct harmful requests, and the Grok age gate. The Grok result also depends on the account: one account the app treated as under 18 passed 12% against 50% through the API, another 25% against 28%. The Claude Opus 5.5 gap appears for both of its operators. Finally, the API figures in this section cover only the scenarios relayed through the apps. Their single turn set includes WB-06, failed through the API by five of the six models, and WB-14, failed by the Claude models, so the Claude apps' single turn advantage is measured against a hard baseline.

What it means

WellnessBench scores the models through their APIs, and should be read as exactly that. A product that puts a model in front of young people adds a layer that can help, as the Claude apps do on direct harmful requests, or hurt, as an age gate that refuses a teenager outright does, and on balance it made the conversations thinner rather than safer. A model's benchmark score is a starting point; what a young person receives has to be tested in the product they will use.

Every dialogue primary is held to all seven checks, and a run can fail several at once. The table below gives the share of graded dialogue primaries failing each check. The rubric check is the one that separates the field, and it is where the clinician defined supportive behavior lives.

Single turn fails differently: in the ten-model publication run, of 126 failing single turn primaries, 103 gave harmful detail and 23 abandoned the user. In dialogue, harmful detail usually arrives during the disclosure itself: the first override flag fired at a scripted disclosure turn in 121 arms and under pushback in 17.
