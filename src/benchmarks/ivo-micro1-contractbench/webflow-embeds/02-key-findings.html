Key findings

  1. 1

    Grok 4.7 leads a close top tier, but completion, cost, and speed separate the leaders.

    Grok leads on weighted rubric pass rate at 58.3%, followed by Opus 5.5 at 58.1% and Kimi K3 at 57.1%. Completion rates range from 80.8% for Opus to 99.5% for Grok and 100% for Kimi. Kimi costs 38% less per completed rollout and finishes 13% faster than Grok.

  2. 2

    Models favor passive decisions, while Astra stands apart on escalation.

    All seven models score higher on opening redlines than on responses to counterparty markups. On Turn 2, models pass more than 96% of criteria requiring them to leave unchanged language alone, compared with 15.1% to 36.5% of criteria requiring a new edit. Astra scores 70.8% on issues requiring internal escalation, more than twice the rate of any other model.

  3. 3

    Models struggle with high-priority issues and context-dense judgment.

    Six of seven models score lowest on issues attorneys consider most important. Every model scores 9 to 15 percentage points lower when company rules must be interpreted against deal-specific facts than when those rules apply unconditionally. Six of seven also score lowest without a company playbook.

  4. 4

    Models score higher where attorney redlines overlap.

    In a controlled study of initial contract reviews, five attorneys independently redline the same agreement, demonstrating multiple viable approaches. Grok scores 67.0% on paragraphs flagged by four or five attorneys, compared with 39.5% on those flagged by only one. Opus scores 65.5% versus 30.3%.

  5. 5

    No model matches attorneys' precision in redlining.

    Attorneys score 63.5% on the surgicality index, which measures the share of targeted inline edits, compared with 36.6% for Opus 5.5, the highest-scoring model. On initial reviews, attorneys make an average of 125 edits per task, each averaging 93 characters, while models make 41 edits averaging 300 characters.

1. Introduction

Contract review is a judgment problem before it is an editing problem. The hard part is not changing language; it is deciding when to intervene at all. A finished redline hides that reasoning, compressing a series of legal, commercial, and authority judgments into the final document. We've built Ivo-micro1 ContractBench to make those judgments visible and measurable at scale.

Ivo-micro1 ContractBench is a fully expert-authored benchmark of 350 contract-review tasks across 35 scenarios and five major agreement types: Master Services Agreement ("MSA"), IP Licensing, Research Partnership, Commercial Lease, and Marketing Agreement. It covers both the first pass of a clean agreement ("Turn 1") and the response to a counterparty markup ("Turn 2"), with variation in commercial context, jurisdiction, and the availability and complexity of enterprise playbooks.

The core innovation is a rubric taxonomy that reconstructs the judgment behind the redline. Each decision is captured as one of four observable actions, Edit, Preserve, Comment, or Escalate, then interpreted together with its priority and the governing playbook and transaction context. This decomposition makes five dimensions of negotiation judgment measurable: whether the reviewer prioritizes the right issues (Issue Prioritization), exercises restraint (Negotiation Restraint), recognizes when to escalate (Escalation Judgment), adapts to the circumstances of the deal (Situational Judgment), and correctly executes the company's standard position (Standard Position Execution).

Ivo-micro1 ContractBench also models contract review as a multi-expert solution space. Instead of assuming that one attorney's redline defines the correct answer, the benchmark captures where experienced attorneys converge and where multiple reasonable approaches exist.

Substantive alignment, however, is only part of a production-grade redline. A model may identify the right issues while producing a document no attorney would send. We introduce a surgicality index and editing-volume measures to quantify how closely models' drafting behavior aligns with attorney practice, capturing differences that substantive rubric scores alone cannot reveal.

The differences in model performance across negotiation judgment, attorney consensus, and drafting behavior demonstrate this benchmark's ability to differentiate model capabilities and provide a durable basis for evaluating future model generations. To our knowledge, Ivo-micro1 ContractBench is the largest and most comprehensive contract review benchmark to date.

Within each agreement type, six scenarios include a company playbook and one does not. Across the 30 playbook scenarios, 19 use a single-agreement playbook and 11 use a higher-complexity multi-contract playbook.

Each scenario combines a contract with transaction-specific commercial context and, where applicable, the represented party's playbook. The playbook defines authorized positions, fallbacks, negotiation rules, and approval boundaries. Each scenario yields five Turn 1 opening-redline tasks and five Turn 2 response tasks, for 175 tasks at each turn. Turn 1 begins from a clean agreement; Turn 2 begins from an attorney-produced counterparty redline.

2.2 Rubric taxonomy

Attorneys complete the redline before writing their rubrics, then encode the most important judgments from the review as approximately 10 to 20 pass-or-fail items per task. Each rubric item identifies the relevant contract section, required action, priority, evaluation dimension, substantive condition required to pass, and a justification documenting the grounding for that judgment.

2.2.1 Required action

Figure 1. Rubric items by required action (share of all items)
  • Edit3,67262.6%
  • Preserve1,51325.8%
  • Escalate60110.3%
  • Comment761.3%

The required action taxonomy has four categories: Edit, Preserve, Comment, or Escalate. For Edit, Preserve, and Comment, the rubric maps directly to the attorney's golden redline. The required action is the action the attorney takes in the document. On Turn 2, Edit further distinguishes Reject, Counter, and New Edit, while Preserve distinguishes Accept, where a counterparty edit should be accepted, from Leave Untouched, where an unedited paragraph should remain unchanged.

Escalate is the one category where the rubric captures a judgment that may not appear in the attorney's golden redline. Attorneys may leave the provision unchanged or resolve the issue in the document, keeping the redline realistic as an outbound, counterparty-ready redline. The rubric separately records whether escalation was required and to whom. Models must identify both in a comment to receive full credit; the ultimate resolution is not scored.

Required action, priority, and grounding together determine the evaluation dimension. Medium- and Low-priority Edit and Comment items measure Situational Judgment when the answer depends on transaction-specific facts, and Standard Position Execution when an unconditional playbook rule supplies the answer.

2.3 Multi-expert solution space

Turn 1 creates a controlled multi-expert solution space. Five attorneys independently review the same contract and grounding materials, each producing a separate redline and rubric set. This yields five expert solutions to the same contract-review problem.

We grade each Turn 1 model output against all five rubric sets. This captures both shared and individualized attorney judgments and lets us measure how model performance changes as attorney consensus increases.

2.4 Agent's environment

All models use the same document-editing environment. They receive the Word contract, the represented party's commercial context and applicable playbook, the task instructions, and the same redlining toolkit.

Turn 1 starts from an unmarked agreement. Turn 2 preserves the counterparty's tracked changes and comments. Models edit the .docx directly using tracked changes, accept or reject decisions, comments, and escalation flags.

2.5 Model evaluation and grading

We evaluate seven models (Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Grok 4.7, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3, and DeepSeek V4 Pro) through the same harness and skill, each at its provider's highest reasoning setting with a per-turn output limit of 128,000 tokens unless the model's documented maximum is lower. We run three independent rollouts for every model input. The 350 benchmark tasks correspond to 210 distinct inputs: 35 shared Turn 1 inputs and 175 distinct Turn 2 counterparty redlines. This produces 630 rollouts per model, including 105 on Turn 1 and 525 on Turn 2. Each Turn 1 rollout is graded against all five attorney rubric sets for the scenario.

For each completed rollout, the grader diffs the returned document against the input contract. A validity gate checks that every modification appears as a tracked change or a permitted accept/reject decision. The grader then creates a normalized change list covering the model's edits by section, the disposition of each counterparty change, its comments and escalations, and the resulting text of each section.

Three judges from different model providers independently grade every rubric item Pass or Fail from that record: Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, and Gemini 3.5 Flash. A strict majority determines the final verdict. Task score equals earned rubric weight divided by total rubric weight, allowing scores to remain comparable across tasks with different numbers of rubric items.

We calculate rubric scores only for completed, gradeable redlines. A rollout that reaches the token limit or fails the validity gate receives no substantive score and instead counts against the model's completion rate.
